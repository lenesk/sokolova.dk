## Danish word stress 

A wrong word stress in Danish may cause misunderstanding or a stronger foreging accent in non-native speakers of Danish, on other words, native speakers can percieve your Danish as more accented, not because of vowels or consonants mispronunciation, but because of moved stress patters. Word stess is also connected with senctence stress and melody, so it has a great value for your Danish to improve stress assignment. In the Danish-Danish dictionaries like https://ordnet.dk or at https://udtaleordbog.dk, you can check the pronuciation of most of the words in Danish, if you have time or practice your presentation for an exam for example. But often there is no time for checking the word stress or when you read aloud you need to procnounce here and now. So some rules about general word stress patterns can beof great help to know. I will refer to those, systematized by Grønnum, Nina (2005) in *Fonetik og fonologi: almen og dansk.* 

  * Word stress can only fall on the syllable with the full vowel, not a reduced one. Like in *selvfølgelig* [sεlˈføljəli], where *[ə]* can not bear stress. Or like in *forståelse* [fʌˈsdɔˀəlsə], where both the first 'e' and the last 'e' are not full, but reduced vowels.
  * If there are more than one full vowel in the word, and one of them is a long one/with glottal stop called 'stød' in Danish, then the syllable with the long vowel will take the stress. Like in *bevægelse*  [beˈvεˀjəlsə], *banegård* [ˈbæːnəˌgɒˀ], *forespørgsel* [ˈfɒːɒsbɶɐ̯səl]. 
  * If there are more than one long vowel in the word, the first syllable with the long vowel will take the stress. Like in *foreskrive*  [ˈfɒːɒˌsgʁiˀvə]. 
 * If there is no long vowels in the word, the last syllable with a short full vowel followed by a consonant will take the stress. Like in *stakit* [sdaˈkid]. 
  * Borrowings from French have stress on the last syllable. Like in *arrangement* [ɑɑŋɕəˈmɑŋ].
  * If ending in a nasal consonant, a word would have the word stress on the last but one syllable.
  * Prefixes, sush as be-, ge-, er-, for-, are usually unstressed. Some of them, such as u-, und-, mis-, van- can be either stressed or not.

Above, I mentioned rules about primary stress. The secondary stress generally is more typical of compounds (komposita) in Danish, i.e. words formed by adding several stems/words together. The general rule about the assignment of the secondary stress in compound words is that the first stem of the compound carries the main stress on that
syllable, which carries the main stress in an isolated pronunciation of the stem. The secondary
stress is assigned to the last stem of the compound on that syllable, which carries the main
stress in an isolated pronunciation of this stem, as for instance, *ˈengelskˌlærer*, *morgenˌmenneske*. However, there are numerous exceptions to this rule.

In Danish the secondary stress is also typical of some suffixes in non-compound words, for example, -dom, -hed, -skab, -som, -vis, -bar, mæssig, -dømme (Grønnum, 2005). Like in *barndom* [ˈbɑːnˌdʌmˀ]. However, the secondary stress generally is more typical of compounds (komposita) in Danish. 

Typycal mispronuciation may occur when non-native speakers pronouns compund words with two main stresses.E.g. in *æbletærte*, the main/primary stress in on *æble*; but a non-native speaker can pronounce the word with two main stresses. Or e.g. in *miljøforurening* the main stress in on *miljø*, not on both stems. Double main/primary stress is possible in Danish, but is not a common feature. It occurs in compound numerals; words, consisting of a preposition and an adverb like in *ˈbagˈefter*, 
*ˈderˈfor*, and some random words *ˈabsoˈlut*, *ˈdiˈrekte*, *ˈalleˈrede*, and adjectives and verbs with the first stem which indicates a very high degree of the second one *ˈisˈkold*, *ˈlynˈhurtig*. A common foreing accent in word stress her would be on the contrary that a single main stress will be given.
